AI leadership, by design

Scaling craft in the agentic age, in service of helping people find their way home.

  • My role: Senior Director, Product Design
  • Team: 25 people: 3 Sr. Leaders, 17 designers, and ~5 contractors20–25 designers, 3 Sr. Managers
  • Key Result:result: ~45 prototypes built monthly. From May to June it was up 1000%.~45 prototypes built monthly.

The challenge

Realtor needed strong Design leadership to define an org-wide, AI transformation.

Realtor needed strong Design leadership to define their AI transformation.

In 2025, weekly AI use among designers jumped from 53% to 91% in a single year, and the average designer went from using three AI tools to seven. Almost every team in the field hit this same inflection point at once. At Realtor, the talent was already in the room. The conditions weren't. No shared way of working, no infrastructure worth building on, no confidence to explore, no rituals that made the team better, and no mandate to look past the next quarter. My goal was to change this.

Problem

Dejected. Morale was at an all time low due to leadership gap.

Problem

Tactical. The team was seen as executional, never strategic.

Problem

Order takers. Requests arrived with no kickoff or xf collaboration.

Problem

Excluded. Strategic planning happened without Design.

Opportunity

Confidence. Design had the opportunity to define what excellence looks like.

Opportunity

Opportunity

Opportunity

My role

Set the right conditions, and the team's best work stops being an accident.

People do their best work when they can see where the team is going and move without waiting for permission. Systems take the repetitive work off the table so judgment has room to operate. Anyone can generate an option now, so the real job is knowing which one deserves to survive.

Building the foundation

One source of truth, orchestrated so every team could build from it.

Figma, GitHub, Style Dictionary, and Zeroheight each held a piece of the truth, and none of them talked to each other. I set the direction to orchestrate them into one system with brand and content guidelines at the base, so the same truth reaches design, engineering, and marketing without anyone stopping to reconcile three versions of it. That orchestration is what turned a component library into infrastructure the whole company runs on, and what gave AI something coherent to build against.

Figma, GitHub, Style Dictionary, and the Zeroheight each held a piece of the truth, and none of them talked. I orchestrated them into one system with brand at the base, so design, engineering, and marketing build from the same source, and AI finally had something coherent to read.

Leaning into cross-discipline expectations

Cross-discipline expectations

When everyone can build, design's job becomes knowing what's worth building.

When everyone can build, the job is knowing what's worth building.

AI handed everyone a credible first draft, so the scarce thing stopped being production and started being judgment. Rather than defend the boundary, I pushed the team through it: designers writing briefs, shipping code, and holding the quality bar on work that didn't originate with them. Then we made that judgment teachable, so a designer on any squad has access to the same standard, and a customer gets the same quality regardless of which team happened to build the screen.

Strategic influence and tactical execution

Strategic influence

Experience visions became how Design earned a say in the strategic roadmap.

My mission was to get Design out of the receiving end of the roadmap and into the room where it gets written, and AI helped us establish and scale the playbook with confidence. It compressed sprints, prototypes, and the presentations around them from weeks to days, so a vision became something we could stand up on demand.

We knew we’d hit the mark when executive leadership started asking for vision sprints by name. Now, customers were getting experiences shaped by design intent instead of by whatever survived the planning cycle.
